Your First Run Club: Pace, Etiquette and What to Expect

September 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: A typical social run club meets at a fixed spot, does a quick briefing and warm-up, then runs 3–5 km in pace groups at a speed where you can still talk. Most finish with coffee or chai. Arrive ten minutes early, tell the host it’s your first time, and join the slowest group. You are very unlikely to be too slow.

The hardest part of joining a run club is the five minutes before the first run: standing at the meeting point, not knowing anyone, wondering whether everyone else is faster. If that’s what’s been stopping you, you’re not alone. Strava’s latest trend report found twice as many Gen Z as Gen X say picking up a new sport can be intimidating.

Here’s what actually happens at a first session, how pace groups work, and the etiquette regulars take for granted, so you can walk in knowing what to expect.

What happens at a typical run club session

WhenWhat’s happening
10–15 min beforePeople arrive, drop bags, chat. The best time to introduce yourself.
Start timeA short briefing: route, pace groups, safety notes. New people are often asked to say hello.
First 5–10 minWarm-up, either a slow jog or some dynamic stretches together.
The runPace groups set off. Usually 3–5 km, often a loop, with regrouping points.
FinishEveryone waits for the last group in. Cheering is normal, not optional.
AfterGroup photo, then coffee or chai. This is where friendships actually start.

Some clubs add a training session midweek, like intervals, hills or a longer Sunday run. For your first visit, pick the social run.

“Am I too slow for run club?”

This is the question almost every beginner searches before their first run. The honest answer: a well-run club is designed so that nobody is too slow. Two things make that work.

  • Pace groups. The group splits by speed, each with a leader who holds a steady pace. The slowest group often includes run-walkers.
  • No-drop policies. Someone, often called the sweeper, stays at the back so nobody finishes alone.

To find your group, use the talk test. At moderate effort you can talk but not sing; at vigorous effort you can only manage a few words before taking a breath. Social runs are meant to be at talking pace. If you’re gasping, drop back a group. Nobody will mind.

If you’re genuinely worried, message the organiser beforehand: “I run about 5K in 40 minutes. Is there a group for me?” Most will say yes, and knowing someone expects you makes it much easier to turn up.

What to wear and bring

  • Shoes you’ve already run in. Your first club run isn’t the day for new shoes.
  • Water, especially from March to June. Not every route passes a tap.
  • Your phone for emergencies, the route and paying for the post-run coffee by UPI.
  • A cap and sunscreen in summer. Something reflective if you’re running before sunrise.
  • A small towel or a change of T-shirt if you’re staying for coffee.

Run club etiquette nobody tells you

  1. Run no more than two side by side on shared paths and park tracks. Walkers, cyclists and other runners need space too.
  2. Call out hazards. A pothole, a dog, a scooter coming the wrong way. Point or shout so the people behind you know.
  3. Stay with your group. Don’t sprint past the pace leader to prove a point. If you want to go faster, move up a group next week.
  4. Stop for the regroup. When the group waits at a corner, wait with them.
  5. Keep headphones quiet, or wear just one earbud, so you can hear instructions and traffic.
  6. Cheer the last runners in. Most clubs have one rule about the finish line: nobody arrives in silence.
  7. Ask before posting photos of other runners, and never share someone else’s route or where they live.
  8. Say thank you to the pace leaders and organisers. Most of them are volunteers.

The social side (yes, including dating)

Run clubs have become one of the most popular ways to meet people in a new city. Strava found 39% more Gen Z than Gen X use fitness to meet people who share their interests. Friendships at a club form slowly and naturally, because you see the same faces every week.

If you’re hoping to meet someone, the etiquette is simple: it’s a running club first. Chat at the coffee stop, not during someone’s hard effort. Take no for an answer the first time. Don’t treat each new member as a prospect. The clubs that stay welcoming are the ones where everyone feels safe coming back.

How to choose the right club

Before you go, check the club’s Instagram or ask the organiser a few questions:

  • What distance and pace does the slowest group usually run?
  • Do you have a no-drop policy or a sweeper?
  • Is it free, or is there a fee or membership?
  • Is the route a loop, or point to point?
  • Are there women-only or beginner sessions?

A club that answers quickly and warmly is usually the same when you turn up.

After your first run

  • Go back at least three times before you decide. Week one is awkward everywhere; by week three people know your name.
  • Swap numbers with one person at your pace. A regular partner turns one club run a week into two or three runs.
  • Train a little between sessions. Two short runs during the week make the club run feel easier fast.

A running group spreads the habit. In a study of 1.1 million runners, friends’ extra kilometres led people to run more themselves. Once you’re in a group, you tend to run more without planning to.

Frequently asked questions

Am I too slow for a run club?

Almost certainly not. Most social run clubs have several pace groups and a no-drop policy, which means someone stays at the back so nobody finishes alone. Message the organiser before you go, ask what the slowest group runs at, and start there. Many clubs welcome run-walkers.

What pace do run clubs run at?

Social runs are usually at conversational pace: fast enough to feel like exercise, slow enough to talk. The exact numbers vary by club, which is why most split into pace groups. If you can talk in full sentences, you’re in the right group.

What should I bring to my first run club?

Comfortable running shoes, water (especially in Indian summers), your phone for UPI payments and emergencies, a light layer or cap depending on the weather, and something reflective if you’re running before sunrise.

What is the etiquette at a run club?

Arrive a few minutes early, introduce yourself to the host, run no more than two side by side on shared paths, call out hazards, stay with your pace group, don’t surge ahead of the leader, and cheer the last people in. Ask before posting photos of other runners.

Is it okay to go to a run club alone?

Yes, and most people do. Tell the host it’s your first time and they’ll usually pair you with a regular. Go back two or three times before you decide whether it’s for you. The first run is always the most awkward.
