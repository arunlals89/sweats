Running in Delhi Pollution: An AQI Guide for Runners

September 27, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: Check India’s National AQI before every outdoor run. Up to 200 (Moderate), most healthy runners can train normally. From 201 to 300 (Poor), keep it short and easy. From 301 (Very Poor) and above, train indoors. In Delhi’s winter, pollution usually peaks around 8–10 am, so late afternoon is often cleaner than dawn. If you have asthma, heart disease or are pregnant, be more cautious and ask your doctor.

Every October, Delhi-NCR runners face the same problem. The weather is finally cool enough for long runs and races, and the air is at its worst. The usual advice to “go early, before the traffic” is often exactly wrong in winter. The idea of quitting until February isn’t much better.

This guide explains how to read the AQI as a runner, when in the day the air is usually cleanest, what the research says about exercising in polluted air, and how to keep training through the season.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant, or are unsure, talk to your doctor before training on high-pollution days.

India’s AQI bands, and what they mean for a run

India’s National Air Quality Index, run by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB), has six bands. The health impact column is CPCB’s own wording. The last column is a practical rule of thumb for healthy adult runners.

AQIBandCPCB health impactFor your run
0–50GoodMinimal impactTrain normally
51–100SatisfactoryMinor breathing discomfort to sensitive peopleTrain normally
101–200ModerateBreathing discomfort to people with lung disease such as asthma, and to people with heart disease, children and older adultsMost people train normally; sensitive groups ease off
201–300PoorBreathing discomfort to most people on prolonged exposureShort and easy; hard sessions indoors
301–400Very PoorRespiratory illness on prolonged exposureTrain indoors; walk rather than run if you go out
401–500SevereAffects healthy people; serious impact on those with existing diseasesSkip outdoor exercise

For context, the World Health Organization’s 2021 guideline for fine particles (PM2.5) is 15 µg/m³ averaged over 24 hours. In Delhi’s post-monsoon and winter months, monitoring stations have recorded seasonal peaks of 300–350 µg/m³, about twenty times that guideline.

The best time to run in Delhi’s winter (it’s probably not dawn)

A 2025 study of CPCB monitoring data from five kinds of location across Delhi (industrial, commercial, residential, traffic and green areas) from 2018 to 2022 found that PM2.5 follows a daily two-peak pattern:

  • A morning peak, around 8–10 am, as traffic and road dust build up.
  • A night peak, around 8 pm to midnight, when the air near the ground stops mixing and traps pollution.

In between, as the sun warms the ground and the lowest layer of the atmosphere mixes more, concentrations usually fall. A six-year study of five Indian megacities found the lowest levels around 3–4 pm, so the afternoon is typically the cleanest part of the day. On a cold, still winter morning, the air at 6 am is often still loaded from the night before.

This is a typical pattern, not a guarantee. Wind, a western disturbance or a crop-burning spike can change it within hours. Treat it as a reason to check the AQI before you set your alarm, not to assume.

What the research says about exercising in polluted air

Two studies are worth knowing, because they point in a more nuanced direction than “never exercise outside”:

  • Exercise usually still wins, up to a point. A 2016 modelling study found that for the global average urban PM2.5 level (22 µg/m³), the benefits of cycling and walking far outweigh the pollution risk. At a constant 100 µg/m³, though, harms began to exceed benefits after about 1 hour 30 minutes of cycling a day. The study only modelled levels up to 200 µg/m³. Delhi’s worst winter days go well beyond that.
  • Pollution shrinks the payoff. A 2025 meta-analysis of 1.5 million adults found that meeting activity guidelines cut the risk of death from any cause by about 30% where PM2.5 was below 25 µg/m³. Where it was 25 µg/m³ or more, the reduction was only 12–15%. Activity still helped, but by less than half as much.

Both studies look at long-term averages, not single runs. The practical point is the same: when the air is bad, how long and how hard you exercise outside matters. When you run hard you breathe several times more air per minute than at rest, and more of it through your mouth, which bypasses your nose’s filtering.

GRAP: when Delhi-NCR tells people to stop

Delhi-NCR’s Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) has four stages that follow the AQI bands: Stage I (Poor), Stage II (Very Poor), Stage III (Severe) and Stage IV (“Severe+”, AQI above 450). At the top stage, restrictions reach sport directly. On 14 December 2025, the Commission for Air Quality Management suspended outdoor physical sports activities and competitions across Delhi-NCR under Stage IV, citing a serious health risk to children.

If schools are being told to stop outdoor sport, take it as your cue to move training indoors too.

A runner’s playbook for pollution season

  1. Check the AQI before every run. Use CPCB’s official Sameer app or the CPCB website, and look at the station nearest your route, not the city average.
  2. Move your run to the cleaner window. On many winter days that means early to late afternoon rather than dawn.
  3. Choose green routes over traffic corridors. In the Delhi study, industrial and traffic areas ran 15–25% higher in PM2.5 than commercial and green areas.
  4. Swap intensity, not just location. On Poor days, keep outdoor runs short and conversational. Save intervals and tempo runs for the treadmill.
  5. Build an indoor week. Treadmill runs, strength sessions, cycling on a trainer, mobility and yoga, ideally in a room with closed windows and a HEPA purifier.
  6. Use a mask for the right job. A well-fitted N95 is useful for walking and commuting. It isn’t a way to make hard running in Severe air safe.
  7. Listen to your body. A cough, chest tightness, unusual breathlessness or a headache is a reason to stop, not to push through.

Who should be extra careful

CPCB’s guidance singles out people with lung disease (such as asthma), heart disease, children and older adults as affected from the Moderate band upwards. Pregnant people should also be cautious. If that’s you, get individual advice from your doctor about exercising on high-AQI days, and keep a reliever inhaler with you if you’ve been prescribed one.

How to keep your training (and streak) on track

The biggest risk of pollution season isn’t a single bad run. It’s losing three months of consistency. A few ways to protect it:

  • Count indoor sessions. A treadmill 5K or a strength workout is still training. Record it like any other workout.
  • Use time-based goals. Minutes challenges keep scoring fair when half the group is on a treadmill and half is outside.
  • Protect your streak. A streak freeze exists for exactly the day when the right decision is not to go out.
  • Move the club indoors. Run clubs can switch to a gym session or a mall walk on GRAP days rather than cancelling. See our run club playbook for more.

Frequently asked questions

What AQI is safe for running?

On India’s National AQI, Good (0–50) and Satisfactory (51–100) are fine for everyone. Most healthy adults can also train normally in the Moderate band (101–200), though people with asthma or heart disease may feel it. From Poor (201+) it makes sense to shorten and ease off, and from Very Poor (301+) to move sessions indoors. This is a rule of thumb, not medical advice.

Is it safe to run when the AQI is 300?

At 300 you’re at the top of the Poor band, where CPCB says most people feel breathing discomfort on prolonged exposure. A short, easy run is a judgement call for healthy adults. Hard intervals and long runs are better done indoors. If you have asthma, a heart condition or are pregnant, stay inside and check with your doctor.

Is early morning the best time to run in Delhi?

Often not in winter. A study of CPCB monitoring data from 2018–2022 found Delhi’s PM2.5 peaks in the morning (8–10 am) and again at night (8 pm to midnight). Levels usually drop in the afternoon as the air mixes. Check the live AQI for your area rather than assuming dawn is cleanest.

Do masks help when running in pollution?

A well-fitted N95 filters most fine particles, but it makes hard breathing uncomfortable and loses its seal when it gets sweaty. It’s useful for walking, commuting or very easy efforts. It doesn’t make hard running in Severe air a good idea.

Is a treadmill better than running outside on high AQI days?

Usually yes, especially if the room has closed windows and a HEPA air purifier. Indoor air isn’t automatically clean, but you control it far more than you control the air on the road.
