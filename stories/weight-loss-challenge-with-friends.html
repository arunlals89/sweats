Weight Loss Challenge With Friends: Rules That Are Fair (and Safe)

September 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: Score weight loss as a percentage of starting weight, not kilos. Cap how much counts each week (about 1%) so crash dieting doesn’t pay, keep weigh-ins private, and give at least half the points for habits like workouts and steps. Run it for 8–12 weeks, and share the prize among everyone who hits their goal.

Weight loss challenges with friends are popular for a good reason: doing it together works. They also go wrong in predictable ways. The biggest person wins because they can lose kilos fastest. Someone crash diets in the final week. Weigh-ins turn into something people dread. Good rules fix all three.

Why doing it with friends works

  • Teams pull each other along. In Shape Up Rhode Island, a 12-week team competition, 3,330 participants lost an average of 4.2% of their starting weight. Results clustered by team, and people who reported more influence from teammates lost more.
  • Shared rewards beat solo rewards. In a randomised trial of 105 employees, a group-based incentive led to more weight loss after 24 weeks than both an individual incentive and monthly weigh-ins alone. Group participants lost 4.4 kg more than the control group.

Why scoring in kilos is unfair

A person who starts at 110 kg can lose 3 kg in a few weeks far more easily than someone who starts at 65 kg. Scoring in kilos means the result is mostly decided on day one. The fix is simple:

% lost = (starting weight − current weight) ÷ starting weight × 100

From 90 kg to 86.4 kg is 3.6 kg, or 4%. From 65 kg to 62.4 kg is 2.6 kg, which is also 4%. Same effort, same score.

Scale weight also swings from day to day with water, salt, food and, for many women, the menstrual cycle. A single weigh-in can be off by more than a week’s real progress. Weigh in once a week, at the same time and under the same conditions (for example, Monday morning before breakfast).

The rules: a template you can copy

  • Length: 8–12 weeks, with a winner each month.
  • Weigh-in: weekly, same day, same time, same scale.
  • Scoring: percentage of starting weight, not kilos.
  • Weekly cap: only up to 1% counts in any one week. Losing faster earns nothing extra.
  • Privacy: only percentages are shared. Actual weights stay private, or go to one trusted scorekeeper.
  • Who sits out: anyone pregnant, under 18, or with a history of disordered eating. People with medical conditions should check with their doctor first.

The weekly cap does the most work. The CDC’s guidance is that people who lose about 1–2 pounds (0.5–1 kg) a week are more likely to keep it off. A cap of around 1% a week keeps the challenge inside that range for most people and removes the reward for extreme dieting.

A points system that rewards habits, not just the scale

The scale is slow and noisy, so a challenge scored only on weight leaves people with nothing to do for six days out of seven. Put at least half of the points on the daily habits that actually drive progress:

ActionPoints
Workout of 30+ minutes10 (max 5 a week)
Hit your daily step goal (your own average + 10%)3 a day
Weekly weigh-in done5
Each 0.25% lost that week (capped at 1%)5 (max 20)
Strength session+5 (max 2 a week)

With this split, someone having a slow week on the scale can still win the week by showing up. That’s what keeps people in the challenge long enough to see real results. Habit-based competition also has good evidence behind it: in the STEP UP trial, a competitive step game added about 920 steps a day, and the effect held after the game ended.

What target to set

Aim for about 5% of starting weight over 8–12 weeks. It sounds modest, but the CDC notes that a 5% reduction can lower the risk of heart disease, prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. For someone starting at 80 kg, that’s 4 kg in 12 weeks, about a third of a kilo a week. That’s very achievable, and it’s the kind of pace that lasts.

Prizes that don’t backfire

  • Shared pot: everyone chips in, and the pot is split among everyone who hits 5%, not just first place. This mirrors the group incentives that worked in the trial above.
  • Group reward: a trek, a day trip or a sports day at the end for everyone who finishes.
  • Small monthly prizes: new running socks, a class pass, first choice of the next group activity.

Avoid winner-takes-all cash pots. They create the strongest incentive to crash diet in the last week, which is exactly what the rules are trying to prevent.

Teams or individuals?

With more than about six people, split into teams of three to five and score each team on its average percentage. Teams add accountability without making anyone’s weight public, and people who don’t want to be the one who let the team down tend to show up for the workouts.

After the challenge

Plan a “maintenance month” straight after. Keep the habit points running, drop the scale scoring, and reward anyone who stays within 1% of their end weight. Most of the value of a challenge is the routine it builds, so don’t let it stop the day the prize is paid out.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate percentage weight loss for a challenge?

Subtract your current weight from your starting weight, divide by your starting weight, and multiply by 100. Someone who goes from 90 kg to 86.4 kg has lost 3.6 kg, which is 4%. Percentages let people of different sizes compete fairly.

What is a realistic goal for a weight loss challenge?

Around 5% of starting weight over 8–12 weeks is realistic for most people, and the CDC notes that a 5% reduction can lower the risk of conditions like heart disease and type 2 diabetes. Its guidance is that losing about 1–2 pounds (0.5–1 kg) a week makes people more likely to keep it off.

How long should a weight loss challenge with friends last?

Eight to twelve weeks. Shorter challenges reward water loss and crash dieting; longer ones lose momentum unless you break them into monthly rounds.

Is a weight loss competition healthy?

It can be, if the rules reward steady habits and discourage extremes. Cap how much weight loss counts each week, keep weigh-ins private, score habits as well as the scale, and leave out anyone who is pregnant, under 18 or has a history of disordered eating.

What are good prizes for a weight loss challenge?

A shared pot that goes to everyone who hits a goal (not just the winner), a group trip or activity at the end, or new running shoes. Avoid food prizes, and avoid winner-takes-all pots that encourage crash dieting in the final week.
